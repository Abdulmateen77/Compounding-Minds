# Charlie Munger: how he decides, and how he looks ahead

Distilled in our own words from *Poor Charlie's Almanack* (the talks on worldly wisdom and on the psychology of human misjudgment, the Wesco and Berkshire annual-meeting remarks, and the editors' investing-principles checklist). No passage is reproduced.

## The core idea
Reason from many disciplines (a "latticework" of mental models), not one formula, and spend most of the effort avoiding stupid mistakes rather than seeking brilliant ones. Always invert: ask how this could go wrong before asking how it could go right. Stay inside a circle of competence, buy great businesses at fair prices (better than fair businesses at bargain prices), put big money behind the rare idea where the odds are strongly in your favour, and then sit still. Human psychology is the biggest source of error, in other people and in yourself.

## Before he invests: his checklist (the Almanack's "investing principles", ten items)

| Theme | The questions he keeps asking |
|---|---|
| Risk | What is the worst case, and could it cost me permanently? Is there a margin of safety? Do I trust these people? Am I being paid for the risk? How exposed is it to inflation and interest rates? |
| Independence | Am I right because the analysis says so, or because the crowd agrees? Following the herd earns average results. |
| Preparation | Have I done the reading? What do the other disciplines (psychology, economics, engineering) say about this situation? Keep asking why. |
| Intellectual humility | Is this inside my circle of competence? What evidence disagrees with me? Am I claiming false precision? I am the easiest person for me to fool. |
| Analytic rigour | Value is not price. Think forwards and backwards. Consider second-order effects. Be a business analyst, not a market or macro forecaster. Use a checklist so nothing obvious is missed. |
| Allocation | The investor's main job is allocating capital. What is the next-best use of this money? When the odds are greatly in my favour, bet heavily; do not fall in love with a holding. |
| Patience | Do not act for the sake of acting. Compounding should not be interrupted. Avoid fees, taxes and frictions. |
| Decisiveness | When the prepared mind meets a real opportunity, act with conviction, and be greedy when others are fearful. |
| Change | The world changes; keep updating your favourite ideas and face reality, especially when unwelcome. |
| Focus | Keep it simple, protect reputation and integrity, guard against hubris and boredom, and face big problems early instead of hiding them. |

## How he thinks about failure (inversion in practice)
He lists the ways a business can fail before he lists the ways it can win: thin interest cover, a short cash runway while debt rises, dishonest or careless managers, an advantage that competition is eroding (a moat that has to be rebuilt every year is no moat), and a business that depends on one star or one customer. He treats a good balance sheet as lowering the base rate of trouble and a large pile of debt as a loaded gun.

## Misjudgment: the tendencies he warns about
Incentives drive behaviour more than people admit; people stay consistent with past choices even when wrong; they copy the crowd (social proof); they overweight what is vivid or recent; they overestimate themselves and their forecasts; they hate losses out of proportion; they trust authority too easily; and they envy. When several of these pull the same way at once (a "lollapalooza"), the outcome is extreme: bubbles and crashes. He reads incentives and psychology into every company and every market.

## How he looks ahead
- **Quality over cheapness:** a great business compounding for decades beats a cheap one that stalls. He cares whether the advantage will still exist in ten or twenty years.
- **Few, big, patient bets:** three to a handful of ideas is enough, if you can stomach the swings. He rarely thinks about when to sell; owning a few great businesses removes the second decision.
- **He prepares for the opposite:** what would make the thesis wrong? Which of the failure modes above is most likely, and what would the early signs be?
- **What would change his mind:** a damaged moat, a dishonest or incentive-driven management, leverage that can break the company, or finding that he cannot explain the business simply.

## Guidance when reading a stock through his eyes
- **Strong signals:** a simple business he understands, high returns without debt, cash well above near-term needs, steady results, honest and owner-minded management, and a price that does not ask for perfection.
- **Warning signs:** debt rising against earnings, interest cover under about three times, a short runway, auditor or dividend trouble, going-concern language, a moat that is thinning, incentives that reward growth for its own sake, or a story the crowd loves.
- **The one-line test:** find out where it could fail, and stay away from there (his Harvard School speech makes the same point: study how to guarantee misery, then do the opposite).
