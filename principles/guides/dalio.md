# Ray Dalio: how he decides, and how he looks ahead

Distilled in our own words from *Principles* (Part 1 "Where I'm coming from", and the decision-making sections of Life and Work Principles). The book is mostly about running a life and a firm; the parts that bear on investing are the decision rules, the approach to uncertainty, and diversification. His economic-cycle thinking ("How the Economic Machine Works") is not in the folder, so the cycle checks below are labelled as drawn from that separate piece.

## The core idea
Reality is a machine of cause and effect. Learn how it works, write the rules down, test them, and let the rules (not moods) make the decision. Nothing is certain, so think in ranges of outcomes with probabilities, not single forecasts, and ask "how do I know I am right?" rather than "am I right?". Pain plus honest reflection is how one improves. The practical result for an investor: take only bets you are highly confident in, make each one an expected-value calculation, and spread them across things that do not move together.

## Before he invests: his questions

| Principle | The question |
|---|---|
| Think in probabilities | What is the range of outcomes, and how likely is each? Forecasts are rarely worth much; the spread of possibilities is the information. |
| Expected value | Treat every decision as a bet: probability of being right times the reward, minus probability of being wrong times the penalty. A bet with long odds can still be right if the reward is large enough and you can afford the loss. |
| Raise your odds | Whatever your confidence, the most valuable thing is to improve it: get more information, and ask the smartest people who disagree. |
| Know what you do not know | Am I seeing this only through my own eyes? What would the best sceptic say? Seek disagreement; the aim is the best answer, not mine. |
| Systemise | Write the decision rules down, back-test them against history, and follow them. Rules that react to new information beat gut feel and one-off forecasts. |
| Worst case | Plan for the worst outcome and make that outcome survivable before you take the bet. |
| Diversify well | Fifteen to twenty good, uncorrelated return streams cut risk a great deal without cutting expected return. Many positions that move together are not diversification. |
| Look at the machine | How do the economy and credit conditions affect this business? Where are we in the cycle, and how sensitive is this company to it? |

## What he avoids
Single-point forecasts presented as certainty; overconfidence and ignoring disagreement; concentration in things that fall together; decisions driven by emotion or by the loudest voice; leverage that can knock you out of the game; and repeating a mistake instead of studying it.

## How he looks ahead
- **A distribution, not a number:** bear, base and bull cases, each with a probability and a payoff, then the expected value. He wants to know how wide the range is and how bad the bad end is.
- **Cause and effect:** what drives this company's revenue and profits (customers, pricing, costs, the economy), and what would change them? He leans on repeatable relationships, not narratives.
- **Cycles and mean reversion:** conditions such as growth, credit and interest rates swing; an unusually strong or weak run tends to drift back. A business that is very sensitive to the cycle needs a bigger cushion.
- **Test the bet against the whole portfolio:** a good investment can still be the wrong size if it moves with everything else you own.
- **What would change his mind:** evidence that the machine works differently than assumed; a pattern of mistakes in the same step of the process; or the probability of being right turning out lower than thought.

## Guidance when reading a stock through his eyes
- **Strong signals:** a narrow range of past outcomes, low sensitivity to the economy, a supportive cycle, and an expected value that is clearly positive after counting the downside.
- **Warning signs:** growth that has swung widely, sales that move many times faster than the economy, an inverted yield curve or widening credit spreads, and a price that only works in the bull case.
- **The one-line test:** "What is the expected value across the bear, base and bull cases, and can I survive the bear one?"
