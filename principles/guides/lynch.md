# Peter Lynch: how he decides, and how he looks ahead

Distilled in our own words from *One Up on Wall Street* (chapters 6 to 17 mainly: the six categories, the perfect stock, stocks to avoid, earnings, the famous numbers, the final checklist). No passage is reproduced.

## The core idea
Know what you own and why. Start with what you can see and understand (a product people love, a business you work in), then do the homework the professionals skip. Decide first which of six kinds of company it is, because each is judged by different numbers and sold for different reasons. Then ask the same simple question of any growth story: is the price fair for the growth? Ignore forecasts of the market and the economy.

## The first step: which kind of stock is it? (ch. 7)

| Type | What it looks like | What he expects | What to watch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Slow grower | Large, ageing, grows about as fast as the economy (2 to 4 percent), pays a generous dividend | Dividend income, little growth | Does the dividend look safe? Not a place for capital gains |
| Stalwart | Big, dependable, grows about 10 to 12 percent a year (consumer staples, big pharma) | A 30 to 50 percent gain, then sell and repeat; protection in recessions | Price against its own history, growth slowing, a rich price |
| Fast grower | Small, aggressive, 20 to 25 percent a year, in a growing niche | The big winners (many times your money) | Balance sheet (debt kills young growers), whether the expansion can be repeated, when growth will end and what you pay for it |
| Cyclical | Sales and profits rise and fall with the economy (cars, airlines, steel, chemicals) | Big gains if bought at the right point in the cycle | Timing is everything: inventories, prices, early signs the cycle is turning. A low P/E at the top can be a trap |
| Turnaround | Battered, losing, sometimes close to bankruptcy | Big, fast gains if it recovers | Can it survive? Debt is the deciding factor: the company must not run out of money before it recovers |
| Asset play | Owns something of value the market has not priced (cash, land, a subsidiary) | The hidden value being recognised | The real size of the asset against the price, and whether management will release it |

## Before he invests: the questions he asks (ch. 8 to 15)
- **Is it fairly priced for its growth?** A company's P/E should be about its earnings growth rate. A P/E at half the growth rate is very attractive; at twice the growth rate, a warning. A refined version adds the dividend yield to growth and divides by the P/E: under 1 is poor, 1.5 is acceptable, 2 or better is what he wants.
- **How strong is the balance sheet?** Debt against equity (a normal company has about 75 percent equity and 25 percent debt). Cash net of debt per share, and what that does to the price you really pay. Among troubled companies, debt decides who survives.
- **What do the products mean to the company?** What share of sales and profits does the exciting product actually represent?
- **Are earnings real and growing?** What drives them (more stores, higher prices, new markets, cost cuts, a turnaround)? Is the growth repeatable?
- **Who owns it, who is buying?** Few big institutions holding it, insiders buying, and the company buying back its own shares are good signs.
- **The "perfect stock" traits:** a dull name and dull business, something people keep buying, a niche with protection, little attention from analysts, a spin-off, bad news already priced in, and managers who own the stock.
- **Is it a final-checklist pass?** By category: for a fast grower, is it still early, can it expand, are earnings growing and the balance sheet strong; for a cyclical, where are we in the cycle; for a turnaround, will it survive.

## What he avoids
The hottest stock in the hottest industry; "the next" famous company; companies that spend heavily on acquisitions outside their business ("diworsification"); anything he cannot explain in two minutes; rumours and tips; and a high P/E with slowing growth.

## How he looks ahead
- **Story first, then numbers:** write down in a couple of sentences why you would own it and what has to happen. Check on the story every few months and sell when the reason is gone, not when the price drops.
- **He reasons by category:** a stalwart that has doubled may be sold; a fast grower is held while its growth story is intact and sold when expansion slows; a cyclical is sold when the cycle turns; a turnaround is judged on survival.
- **Time horizon:** years, not months. The big gains come from companies that keep growing for a long time, so the point is to own them while the growth lasts and not to sell the winners too early.
- **What would change his mind:** growth slowing while the price still assumes it, a balance sheet that weakens, the story turning out to be false, or the stock becoming the market's darling.

## Guidance when reading a stock through his eyes
- **Strong signals:** a clear category, growth that matches or exceeds the P/E, low debt, net cash, and an unexciting business people keep buying.
- **Warning signs:** a P/E well above the growth rate, rising debt, a growth story that depends on a single product, a hot stock in a crowded field, or a cyclical bought at peak earnings.
- **The one-line test:** "Can I explain, in two minutes, why I own it and what would make me sell?"
