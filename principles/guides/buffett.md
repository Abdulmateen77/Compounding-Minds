# Warren Buffett: how he decides, and how he looks ahead

Distilled in our own words from *The Warren Buffett Way* (Robert Hagstrom; chapters 5 to 11), checked against the Berkshire sources in `../SOURCES.md`. No passage is reproduced; page references are to the printed book.

## The core idea
He buys a piece of a business, not a ticker. He asks three things in order: is this a business I can understand and trust to look much the same in ten years, are the people running it honest and sensible with the owners' money, and can I buy it for clearly less than it is worth. Price is what you pay; value is what you get. He does not predict markets, economies or sectors, and he skips any company whose future cash he cannot estimate with confidence.

## Before he invests: the twelve questions (the "tenets", ch. 5 to 8)

| Group | Question | What it means in practice |
|---|---|---|
| Business | Is it simple and inside my circle of competence? | If you cannot explain how it makes money and what could break it, you cannot value it. Success comes from defining what you do not know. |
| Business | Is the operating history consistent? | A long record of the same business doing the same thing predicts better than a story about change. He distrusts turnarounds and big strategy shifts: severe change and exceptional returns rarely come together. |
| Business | Are the long-term prospects favourable? | He wants a franchise: something people need or want, with no close substitute, and not price-regulated. That gives pricing power, a "moat", and the hope of being great for 25 to 30 years. A commodity seller can only win by being the lowest-cost producer. |
| Management | Is management rational? | Above all: what does it do with spare cash? Reinvest only if it earns more than the cost of capital; otherwise return it (dividends, buybacks). Reinvesting at poor returns is common and irrational. |
| Management | Is it candid with shareholders? | Does it own up to mistakes, report the bad with the good, and avoid "adjusted" numbers that hide costs? A habit of always "hitting the numbers" eventually becomes a habit of inventing them. |
| Management | Does it resist the institutional imperative? | Does it think for itself, or copy rivals, chase acquisitions, and let the budget expand to the cash available? |
| Financial | Return on equity? | Operating earnings over shareholders' equity, judged on four- or five-year averages, excluding one-off gains, and without leaning on debt. EPS growth alone proves little, because retained profit grows equity automatically. |
| Financial | "Owner earnings"? | Net income plus depreciation and amortisation, minus the capital spending needed just to stay where it is (and any working-capital build). Cash flow that ignores capex flatters. |
| Financial | Profit margins? | Good businesses turn sales into profit, and low-cost operators keep finding costs to cut every day, not in a once-a-decade restructuring. |
| Financial | The one-dollar premise | Over time each dollar of retained profit should create at least one dollar of market value. It is a quick test of whether management reinvests well. |
| Value | What is it worth? | Discount the owner earnings it will produce for years to come. He wants predictable earnings and uses a rate around 10% when bond yields are low. If he cannot estimate them, he passes. |
| Value | Is the price clearly below value? | A margin of safety. He will pay up for quality, but never in a way that needs everything to go right. |

## What he avoids
Businesses he cannot understand (he skipped technology for decades); hot stocks and fashions; companies in the middle of reinventing themselves; heavy debt, especially debt used to flatter returns; managers he does not trust, however attractive the numbers; and paying a price that needs ten years of perfect execution.

## How he looks ahead
- **Time horizon:** think of a ten-year hold with no way to change your mind. Would you still want to own it? Anything under five years is a fool's game. Turnover of 10 to 20 percent a year, a hold of five to ten years.
- **What has to stay true:** the moat is durable, the returns on capital stay high without more debt, and the people stay honest. He watches for erosion of the moat (pricing power, margins, a rising need for capital) more than for the next quarter.
- **How he handles uncertainty:** he does not forecast the economy. He prefers businesses whose earnings are as predictable as a bond's, and builds the safety into the price he pays.
- **Concentration:** put real money into a few ideas he knows well (ten to twenty names at most, the best ones heavily). Price swings are noise if the business is sound; he defines risk as the chance of permanent harm, not volatility.
- **What would change his mind:** a damaged moat, management behaving badly, capital being wasted, or a price far above value.

## Guidance when reading a stock through his eyes
- **Strong signals:** consistent profits, high returns on equity and on capital without leverage, steady or rising margins, free cash flow near or above net profit, falling or low debt, a clear moat, and a price at or below a conservative value.
- **Warning signs:** margins sliding while capital spending climbs, cash profit lagging accounting profit, rising debt, results that swing widely year to year, a business that keeps "changing direction", or a share price that already assumes everything goes right.
- **The one-line test:** "Would I be comfortable owning this for ten years if the market closed tomorrow?"
