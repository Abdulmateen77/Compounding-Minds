# Rakesh Jhunjhunwala: how he decided, and how he looked ahead

Distilled in our own words from your note "Rakesh Jhunjhunwala's 10 Investing Commandments" The note itself lists items to verify (starting capital, the Titan P/E, some order-book numbers). The ideas are useful as a way of thinking; the numbers are not screening rules until checked. He built his record in Indian markets, so where a rule needs data we do not have for US filings, the check says so.

## The core idea
Back long-term outperformers run by honest leaders, buy them at a sensible price, and hold with conviction through noise. Decide on data (growth, returns, cash, who owns the stock), not on opinion or media, and always ask why a company earns its profit. Character and compliance come before valuation: a good story run by someone you cannot trust is a loss waiting to happen.

## Before he invests: the ten commandments

1. **Buy stocks that can outgrow the market.** Aim for growth well above an index's ordinary pace (the note cites about 24 percent a year against about 12 percent), sustained over several years, not a one-year spike.
2. **Back the leader, not just the company.** A strong leader can rescue a weak company and a weak one can wreck a strong company. Governance failures and unmanaged debt are the cautionary tales.
3. **Look for near-monopolies.** Roughly half or more of a market means pricing power, steadier margins and stable growth.
4. **Price matters more than popularity.** A great company at a fair price beats a popular one at a high price. He avoided hyped stocks at very high P/Es and preferred low ones, compared inside the same sector (norms differ by industry).
5. **Choose captive, always-needed demand.** Customers should come to the company, in every economic condition. A strong brand becomes the name of the category.
6. **Check promoter and management character first.** Honesty, compliance, payment record, accounting, debt. His own large loss came from researching the business and not the people.
7. **Prefer strong free cash flow.** Cash after capital spending lets a company repay debt, pay dividends and reinvest without borrowing, even in a downturn.
8. **Decide on data: the three-pillar check.** Promoters holding more than about half (skin in the game), consistent revenue growth, and return on capital employed above about 20 percent.
9. **Prefer low retail and high institutional ownership.** Institutions hold long and add steady demand; retail holders tend to panic in swings.
10. **Favour a large order book.** A big backlog gives visibility on future revenue and signals demand.

## What he avoided
Hyped stocks at very high P/Es; companies run by leaders of doubtful honesty; heavy or unmanaged debt; weak governance and poor accounting; businesses without pricing power; and decisions based on tips, media noise, or short-term trading.

## How he looked ahead
- **Multi-year compounding:** he wanted businesses that could grow far above the index for many years, and he held through setbacks (the note's Titan example: profits fell and unions struck, but revenue kept growing and costs were being cut, so he stayed).
- **Visibility:** order books, brand-driven demand and market share make future revenue more predictable.
- **Leadership as the leading indicator:** the quality and honesty of the leader is the thing to watch most closely over time.
- **What would change his mind:** a governance or accounting red flag, debt that is not under control, a leader he stops trusting, or a business losing its pricing power.

## Guidance when reading a stock through his eyes
- **Strong signals:** sustained growth well above the market, high returns on capital, strong free cash flow, low debt, a dominant position, and a sensible price against its sector.
- **Warning signs:** a hyped price, rising debt, accounting oddities, slowing growth, or leadership controversy.
- **The one-line test:** "Do I trust the person running it, and do the numbers (growth, returns, cash) say the same thing as the story?"
